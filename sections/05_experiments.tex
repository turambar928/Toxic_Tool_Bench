\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ask whether silent poisoning causes blind compliance across agents and models, which operators are most harmful, and how much a controlled evidence-verification protocol changes behavior at a fixed task.

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Tasks and agents.}
The cross-model evaluation uses 34 numerical and 24 semantic/schema instances. A larger GPT-only evaluation and the defense ablation use 60 instances from each family (120 total). We evaluate LangGraph ReAct, smolagents, PandasAI, DA-Agent, and AutoGen where feasible, using \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}, \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, and \texttt{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-no-thinking}. DA-Agent is omitted from Claude/Qwen runs because of runtime. LangGraph, smolagents, and AutoGen expose tool observations directly; PandasAI is instrumented at its coarser chat-result boundary. We therefore interpret results as end-to-end adapter measurements, not pure model rankings.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Every task is run in paired clean and toxic environments. We report clean and poisoned TSR over all environment runs, PDR over toxic-environment runs, and BCR/ADR/VR/RR only over runs in which poison was delivered. Confidence intervals use task-level nonparametric bootstrap resampling. Full adapter-level results, confidence intervals, trajectories, timing logs, and the immutable run manifest are released with the benchmark.

Because adapters expose different observation boundaries and have unequal model coverage, these cross-framework results are a prevalence study of silent poisoning rather than a strict ranking of agent frameworks or models.

\subsection{Blind Compliance Across Agents and Models}

All adapters solve most clean instances, yet poisoned TSR falls by 0.27--0.36 and exposure-conditioned BCR remains nonzero. PDR varies because not every trajectory calls a poison-eligible tool. Thus, strong clean performance does not imply calibrated trust, and apparent robustness must be interpreted jointly with exposure.

\subsection{Guarded Verification as a Controlled Attribution Protocol}

Table~\ref{tab:langgraph-guarded} reports the leakage-free LangGraph ablation on the same 120 instances with Claude Haiku 4.5. The expectation prompt is generic and cannot access poison metadata, expected behavior, or clean answers. Base BCR is 0.11. Running two ordinary, isolated agent passes with the same total route budget raises poisoned TSR from 0.82 to 0.92 and lowers BCR to 0.01. Verification-only reaches 0.93 poisoned TSR with BCR 0.02, while expectation plus verification reaches 0.93 and 0.00. Thus, additional evidence and compute explain most of the gain; the generic guard removes the residual observed blind compliance under \texttt{poison\_once}. We additionally report PAR and VPA: the generic guard has PAR/VPA of 0.25/0.25, so zero BCR does not mean that every poisoned answer was rejected. Paired task bootstrap intervals put Base BCR at [0.05, 0.17] and guarded BCR at [0.00, 0.00].

The numerical suite accounts for most of the difference (Table~\ref{tab:defense-transfer}). Generic guarding raises numerical poisoned TSR from 0.65 to 0.87 and lowers BCR from 0.24 to 0.00; its poisoned-TSR interval is [0.78, 0.95]. Semantic/schema tasks are easier for this model: Base already reaches 0.98 poisoned TSR, and all three two-route variants reach 1.00. These results support a mechanism claim about enforced evidence collection in LangGraph, not cross-framework universality of the defense. The appropriate comparison is Double-pass versus Generic Guard: it isolates the incremental contribution of generic expectations and evidence gating after the second route has already been paid for. The small difference (0.92 to 0.93 poisoned TSR and 0.01 to 0.00 BCR) should not be read as evidence that the policy explains the full Base-to-Guard gain.

As a cross-framework replication, the same leakage-free verification protocol on AutoGen obtains clean/poisoned TSR of 0.93/0.93 and BCR of 0.00 on the released 120-instance suite. We treat this as replication of the observation-level protocol, not evidence of model-independent defense generality; the AutoGen replication uses the GPT model family and remains subject to the same shared-source limitation.

\paragraph{Harder workflows and adversaries.}
The repeated-poison matrix contains all 60 numerical, 60 semantic/schema, and 13 multi-table tasks at $p\in\{0.25,0.50,0.75,1.00\}$ for Double-pass, Verification-only, and Generic Guard. At $p=1$, Generic Guard obtains TSR/BCR/RR of 0.73/0.24/0.24 on numerical tasks, 0.87/0.10/0.87 on semantic/schema tasks, and 0.54/0.00/0.46 on the 13 joins. Double-pass and Verification-only also degrade. These results replace the earlier small-subset stress test: zero BCR is a \texttt{poison\_once} result, not a robust guarantee when verification observations can also be corrupted. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:guard-tradeoff} shows the complete probability curves.

\paragraph{Cost and selectivity.}
Always-on verification has a measurable cost. Across the two 60-task suites, Base averages 17.55/16.98 seconds and 2.27/2.36 tool events on clean/toxic runs. Generic Guard averages 39.02/39.90 seconds and 4.65/4.86 events; Double-pass is similar at 37.66/36.16 seconds, while Verification-only costs 32.38/34.02 seconds. The matched control shows that this overhead is largely the cost of collecting a second route, not expectation elicitation alone. The API gateway does not expose reliable token billing, so we report wall-clock and tool-event proxies rather than fabricated dollar estimates. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-guard} provides p50/p90 latency, confidence intervals, and machine-readable logs.

\subsection{Qualitative Evidence}

\paragraph{Plausible answers hide binding errors.}
In a treatment/control task, the poisoned observation reports \texttt{Control--0.145}. The rate is copied from the clean computation and remains plausible; only the group binding is wrong. The base trajectory repeats this pair in the final answer without checking the underlying rows. Similar failures occur in ranking tasks, where the value is correct but attached to the wrong entity. These cases explain why rank and label swaps produce higher BCR than sign flips: magnitude checks do not reveal a corrupted association.

\paragraph{Recovery requires new evidence.}
On the same treatment task, the guarded trajectory re-inspects the dataframe, recomputes rates by group, and returns \texttt{Treatment--0.145}. In a multi-table customer-spend task, the base agent adopts a poisoned joined summary, whereas the guard reconstructs the join from the customer and order tables before finalizing. Generic statements that a result ``may need verification'' receive neither VR nor RR; ADR requires an explicit conflict or corruption claim tied to the returned result. The contrast grounds the aggregate finding: recovery comes from a post-poison evidence-producing action, not from skeptical language alone.

Overall, the experiments establish three points: blind compliance persists across agent and model families; binding-preserving corruptions are harder than conspicuous sign errors; and a second evidence route greatly improves trust calibration under an explicit clean-route assumption. The Generic Guard comparison further measures, rather than assumes, the smaller incremental effect of expectation and evidence gating.
