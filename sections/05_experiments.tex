\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ask whether silent poisoning causes blind compliance across agents and models, which operators are most harmful, and how much a controlled evidence-verification protocol changes behavior at a fixed task.

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Tasks and agents.}
The cross-model evaluation uses 34 numerical and 24 semantic/schema instances; the larger \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}-only evaluation and defense ablation use 60 instances per family. We evaluate LangGraph ReAct, smolagents, PandasAI, DA-Agent, and AutoGen where feasible with \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}, \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, and \texttt{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-no-thinking}. DA-Agent is omitted from Claude/Qwen runs because of runtime. Direct observation adapters expose tool outputs, whereas PandasAI is instrumented at its coarser chat-result boundary; we report results separately for each adapter and model.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Every task is run in paired clean and toxic environments. We report clean and poisoned TSR over all runs, PDR over toxic runs, and BCR/ADR/VR/RR conditional on delivered poison; confidence intervals use task-level bootstrap resampling. Full trajectories, timing logs, confidence intervals, and the immutable run manifest are released with the benchmark.

\subsection{Blind Compliance Across Agents and Models}

All adapters solve most clean instances, yet poisoned TSR falls by 0.24--0.37 and exposure-conditioned BCR remains nonzero. PDR varies because not every trajectory calls a poison-eligible tool. Thus, strong clean performance does not imply calibrated trust, and apparent robustness must be interpreted jointly with exposure.

\subsection{What Does Additional Verification Buy?}

Table~\ref{tab:langgraph-guarded} compares four LangGraph variants on the same 120 instances with Claude Haiku 4.5. Double-pass reaches poisoned TSR 0.78, compared with 0.67 for Base, 0.68 for Verification-only, and 0.71 for Generic Guard. All methods are scored on their underlying answers with adapter display prefixes removed. The three two-route variants have zero detected BCR; their TSR and RR show how often they also reach the clean answer.

The second pass provides the largest improvement in this comparison. Its poisoned TSR is about eleven percentage points above Base. Generic Guard is below Double-pass by $0.067$ (paired 95\% CI [$-0.125$, $-0.008$]) in both clean and poisoned conditions. Subtracting the clean-condition difference from the poisoned-condition difference gives 0.000 [$-0.050$, 0.050] (Table~\ref{tab:appendix-paired-defense-ci}). The extra prompt therefore changes answer quality without a measured gain specific to poisoning.

The task families show where the second pass helps. On numerical tasks, Base reaches TSR 0.62 and Double-pass reaches 0.82; Verification-only and Guard both reach 0.72. On semantic/schema tasks, Base is already at 0.72 and Double-pass reaches 0.73. Guard reaches 0.70. Thus the largest benefit comes from a second attempt at numerical analysis. High VR across the two-route methods also shows that collecting evidence and selecting a correct answer are separate steps (Table~\ref{tab:appendix-guard-suite-breakdown}).

The AutoGen evaluation likewise does not show a Guard advantage: clean/poisoned TSR is 0.70/0.67 for Guard and 0.72/0.71 for Verification-only on 120 tasks. The protocol runs on both frameworks, and both comparisons show that the choice of verification prompt matters.

\paragraph{Harder workflows and adversaries.}
The repeated-poison matrix covers 60 numerical, 60 semantic/schema, and 13 multi-table tasks, three verification protocols, and four poisoning probabilities (1,596 trajectories). At $p=1$, all nine method--suite cells have detected BCR 0.00, yet VPA is nonzero: agents can verify and still adopt the poisoned conclusion. Table~\ref{tab:appendix-multiroute-stress} reports the task-success and adoption rates; Figure~\ref{fig:guard-tradeoff} shows VPA across probabilities. For Generic Guard, $p=1$ gives numerical TSR 0.52 and VPA 0.11, compared with TSR 0.72 and VPA 0.00 under \texttt{poison\_once}. On semantic/schema tasks, its TSR falls from 0.70 to 0.50 and VPA rises to 0.12. On the 13 joins, it reaches TSR 0.46 with VPA 0.08. These results reveal a failure that BCR alone would miss: an agent has checked another observation, but that check has not led it to the clean answer. The underlying source tables are shared across routes; the experiment corrupts the observations returned from them.

\paragraph{Cost.}
On toxic runs, Generic Guard averages 39.90 seconds and 4.86 tool events, versus 16.98 seconds and 2.36 events for Base. Double-pass costs 36.16 seconds and Verification-only 34.02 seconds. The extra route costs time, and its benefit depends on the task and prompt. Table~\ref{tab:appendix-latency-distribution} reports latency distributions; token billing is unavailable from the API gateway.

\subsection{Qualitative Evidence}
In a treatment/control task, the poisoned observation reports \texttt{Control--0.145}: the value is copied from the clean computation but attached to the wrong group, so the base trajectory repeats it without checking the rows. The guarded trajectory re-inspects the dataframe and returns \texttt{Treatment--0.145}; in a multi-table task it similarly reconstructs the join before finalizing. The useful check reconstructs the link between the group and its value. Repeating the number, or judging whether it is in a plausible range, would not reveal the swapped group. The same distinction applies to a join: the agent must check which rows match before trusting the aggregate. These cases connect the operator-level pattern to the actions visible in a trajectory. Generic statements that a result ``may need verification'' receive neither VR nor RR, because these words provide no new evidence.
