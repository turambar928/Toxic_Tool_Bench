\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

ToxicBench exposes a reliability gap that ordinary tool-use accuracy misses: an agent may call the right tool, receive a valid response, and still copy false evidence into its answer. Across frameworks and model families, high clean-task success coexists with substantial degradation and blind compliance under numerical and semantic/schema poisoning. In the leakage-free LangGraph ablation, the two-route variants produce different poisoned-task success profiles, but the paired intervals do not establish a guard-specific improvement. The revised scorer detects no Generic Guard BCR-positive trajectory under \texttt{poison\_once}; because its development audit has limited BCR recall, this does not establish the absence of human-judged blind compliance.

The result has a specific scope. Frameworks expose different poisoning boundaries, with PandasAI measured at a coarser chat-result interface, so absolute adapter rankings are not fully comparable. The cross-model suites are smaller than the expanded GPT-only suite, and the benchmark still emphasizes CSV and tabular workflows despite the multi-table extension. Larger databases, SQL services, live APIs, and notebook workflows remain important tests.

The current benchmark is a controlled fault-injection study rather than a corpus of production incidents. Its operators are motivated by realistic failure modes such as pipeline bugs, stale metadata, API-like drift, and biased retrieval, but they are implemented as reproducible proxies and are not intended to estimate field incidence. The expanded suite contains 120 single-table instances and the join extension contains 13 tasks; these sizes support paired trajectory analysis but do not cover the diversity of enterprise data systems. We therefore present ToxicBench as an extensible protocol whose external validity should be tested with larger datasets, live interfaces, and incident-derived corruptions under appropriate release licenses.

The defense also assumes redundancy. Its main \texttt{poison\_once} setting is a mechanism analysis: it allows a new execution route to recover clean evidence, but both routes share the same source tables and backend. Source-independent coverage is therefore 0/133, and our full 133-task repeated-poison stress matrix shows that recovery falls and BCR can return as more verification observations are corrupted. These experiments do not model a poisoned source, parser, or shared cache, and the paper makes no guarantee for simultaneous corruption of all routes. Always-on verification roughly doubles both tool events and latency. Better systems should combine source-diverse provenance, contracts, and calibrated risk signals to trigger verification selectively. ToxicBench provides paired tasks, trajectory metrics, and controlled corruption needed to evaluate those policies. The deterministic scorer is reproducible but imperfect; an independent two-annotator audit over 120 trajectories yields pre-adjudication macro Cohen's $\kappa=0.913$. A subsequent blind adjudication resolves all 16 disputed label cells. Under the revised rules, scorer precision/recall/F1 are 0.750/0.250/0.375 for BCR and 1.000/0.030/0.059 for ADR; VR and RR are also imperfect. We therefore treat automatic behavior rates as operational proxies and release the revision-impact table with the raw trajectories.
