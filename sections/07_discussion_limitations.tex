\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

ToxicBench exposes a reliability gap that ordinary tool-use accuracy misses: an agent may call the right tool, receive a valid response, and still copy false evidence into its answer. Across frameworks and model families, high clean-task success coexists with substantial degradation and blind compliance under numerical and semantic/schema poisoning. In the leakage-free LangGraph ablation, requiring a second evidence route substantially improves poisoned-task success. A matched-compute control captures most of this benefit, while Generic Guard removes the residual observed blind compliance under \texttt{poison\_once}.

The result has a specific scope. Frameworks expose different poisoning boundaries, with PandasAI measured at a coarser chat-result interface, so absolute adapter rankings are not fully comparable. The cross-model suites are smaller than the expanded GPT-only suite, and the benchmark still emphasizes CSV and tabular workflows despite the multi-table extension. Larger databases, SQL services, live APIs, and notebook workflows remain important tests.

The defense also assumes redundancy. Its main \texttt{poison\_once} setting allows a new execution route to recover clean evidence, but both routes share the same source tables and backend. Our full 133-task repeated-poison stress matrix shows that recovery falls and BCR can return as more verification observations are corrupted; it still does not model a poisoned source, parser, or shared cache. Always-on verification roughly doubles both tool events and latency. Better systems should combine source-diverse provenance, contracts, and calibrated risk signals to trigger verification selectively. ToxicBench provides paired tasks, trajectory metrics, and controlled corruption needed to evaluate those policies. The deterministic scorer is reproducible but imperfect; the released two-annotator packet is designed to quantify this residual uncertainty once independent labels are complete.
